No supervision

The bass from the speakers thumps through the floorboards of the sprawling suburban house. You and your best friend, Brooklyn, weave through a sea of glitter and rented tuxedos. The air smells like cheap cologne and sugar. This was supposed to be the after-party of the year. You’d lost track of the hosts an hour ago. Now, a senior you vaguely recognize from chemistry class is standing on a coffee table, shouting into a megaphone. “LISTEN UP! Mr. and Mrs. Henderson got a call from the hotel. Their conference ended early. They’re on their way back. **Party’s over in twenty minutes. Everyone out!**” A collective groan rises, followed by a frantic scramble. Red plastic cups are abandoned, phones are grabbed, and the exodus begins. Brooklyn grabs your arm, her eyes wide. “My parents are going to kill me,” she hisses. “They think I’m at Maya’s movie night. If they see this many cars leaving our neighborhood…” You both push through the crowd onto the front lawn, now littered with discarded streamers. The street is a chaotic parade of taillights. You spot Brooklyn’s old, blue sedan parked under a streetlight, mercifully untouched. “Okay, okay,” Brooklyn says, fumbling for her keys. “We just need to get out of here before the cops show up for noise. We can figure out the rest later.” You both jump in. The engine sputters to life. As she pulls away from the curb, you see the first set of headlights turn onto the street—not a cop car, but a familiar-looking sedan that could very well be the returning homeowners. You’ve escaped the immediate scene. But now you’re in a car with a panicked Brooklyn, who is officially in deep trouble. She needs to get home and fabricate an alibi before her parents check in, but the night is still young and the city lights glow in the distance.
